# The FOG library - a curated compendium (#207, #208)

**Status: Solved.** The compendium is a reference and it is measured end to end
from the extracted bytes — 231 archives, 231 disks, 5,430 catalogue entries; what
it does not settle is two members of the 5,987 that no decompressor recovers, and
the 1,142 places where the disks' own pages and the disks disagree.

The **First Osborne Group** (FOG) disk library, extracted and catalogued.
**231 archives, 231 disks, 5430 catalogue entries.**  Every number here is measured from the
extracted bytes; nothing is taken from `fog-index.htm`, whose CRC column does not
reproduce.  Generated by `tools/research/fog_catalogue.py` and
`tools/research/fog_commentary.py`; the per-file data is in
`docs/fog-library-catalogue.tsv` beside this file.  (5,987 files were extracted;
5,430 of them are described on a disk's own catalogue page, which is where
these entries come from.)

The headline finding is in the dates on the disks: the library records its own
platform's decline.  Of the 11 disks dated 1985, **7 are Osborne-specific**; of
the 10 dated 1991, **4** — the rest are Kaypro, Morrow, Commodore, Televideo and
Superbrain (*The library records its own platform's death*, below).

## Notes on the catalogue - from the extraction session

### Why any of this exists

In 1985 there was no internet, no package manager, and no way to look at software
before you had it. A user group's disk library *was* the distribution channel:
you posted a blank disk and return postage to a P.O. box in Daly City, and
software came back. FOG was, quite literally, an App Store made of envelopes.

Three things here are direct consequences of that, and once you see them the
library reads differently:

1. **Every disk carries its own catalogue page.** You could not browse before you
   bought, so the listing *was* the product: a file table with CRCs and a
   description of each file, written by a volunteer for a stranger. That is why
   the pages are the best documentation in the archive -- and why they are also
   sometimes wrong. They were maintained by hand, at the edge of a 182 KB budget,
   for six years.
2. **`<SHAREWARE>`** appears on 54 of the 219 disks, with FOG's own explanation
   that the program is "distributed on a 'try first, pay if you like it' basis".
   That is the honour-system licence, decades before anyone called it shareware,
   and it is why so much of this survives at all.
3. **The compression-marker extensions.** Files appear on the disks as `.AQM`,
   `.IQC`, `.PQS`, `.ZZ0` -- not errors, but the CP/M schemes' markers for a
   Squeezed or Crunched member, where the second letter of the extension is
   replaced by `Q`. FOG's pages list the *stored* names; the extracted disks carry
   the restored ones. 477 of the apparent page/disk mismatches are this.

   **And it is not only bookkeeping.** A selection list written from the pages
   matches nothing for those files: 27 entries of the drive's own list (#262)
   were dropped in silence that way on 2026-10-23, among them `M7OD-5.AQM` --
   the overlay that makes MDM740 usable with this machine's Comm-Pac -- and
   `MLOAD24.COM`, the program that installs MDM7, MEX and IMP overlays at all.
   `tools/research/populate-hd.py` indexes both spellings now, and stages the
   file under the name the disk actually has.

### What to show someone born long after the Osborne 1 was obsolete

Not WordStar -- they can find WordStar. The value here is the long tail that
exists nowhere else, and the way in is to read the *prose*:

- **FOGLN910** -- an 8080 instruction-set emulator, written in 1982 as a teaching
  tool by a technical college. Someone taught assembly language on an Osborne 1
  by emulating the chip the Z80 replaced.
- **FOGLG5-6** -- a complete Algol for CP/M, with a 25 KB user manual and a test
  suite. Nobody asked for Algol on an Osborne 1.
- **FOGLG1-2** and **FOGLG3-4** -- a Z80 assembler and a C compiler, *with the
  compiler's own source*, split across two library disks.
- **FOGLNG11** -- MVP-FORTH, the public-domain FORTH-79, with assembler source.
- **FOG044** -- FLIPKEYS, a 1985 utility that flips the arrow keys between the
  WordStar and CP/M conventions. The Osborne 1 *has* arrow keys -- they are in the
  keyboard matrix; what it lacks is agreement between two pieces of software about
  what they send.
- **FOG219** -- a Dungeons game patched for a 52-column screen.

### The library records its own platform's death

Measurable, and the most interesting thing in the catalogue. Grouping the disks
by the year on their own pages:

| year | disks | Osborne-specific | other platforms |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1985 | 11 | **7** | none |
| 1987 | 23 | 8 | Kaypro, Commodore, generic CP/M |
| 1988 | 9 | 0 | Morrow, Kaypro |
| 1991 | 10 | 4 | Morrow x2, Kaypro, Superbrain, Commodore, Televideo |

In 1985 the library is almost entirely Osborne 1. By 1991 only a minority of
disks are, and the rest are Kaypro, Commodore C-128, Morrow, Televideo and
Superbrain. **FOG outlived the Osborne 1 by ceasing to be about it** -- a group
named after a dead computer became a general CP/M group, and this is the evidence.

### Two things worth knowing before trusting it

- **The index is not a source.** `fog-index.htm`'s CRC column does not reproduce:
  it claims `CAL2-EX.COM` is `4A98` and the file on the disk is CRC-16/ARC `3AF9`.
  It covers 149 of the 219 disks. Useful as a cross-check, bad as an oracle.
- **The disks' own pages are not a source either.** 573 files they list are not on
  the disks, and 569 files on the disks are not listed -- 477 of the latter
  differing only by a compression-marker extension. Only the extracted bytes are
  trustworthy, which is why this catalogue was built from them.

## Notes on the catalogue - from the maintainer

**This is the factory library, not a side archive.** The O1NEXT ships with onboard
library flash, and this is what goes on it -- so the catalogue is a shipping
manifest, not a curiosity. It's the bootstrap to "do something cool" that everyone
looks for. The Research Assistant focused on the languages and compilers. I'm here
for the GAMES! The vintage of me who had an Osborne 1 and was trying to cross
format games from printouts on other platforms just so I'd have *something* to
play is VERY excited about running through all of these!

**The hook is the machine, not the disk.** Nobody is persuaded by "will my HFE
work". They are persuaded by power on -> `A>` -> `DIR` -> WordStar -> print ->
PDF, with no floppy in the machine. That's a technically slick thing that is
enabled by the modern hardware... but of course modern hardware can do technically
slick things. This library shows you in extraordinary detail what was pioneering
on these machines - things that you take for granted that appear as separate
utilities or programs but are minor functions of your every day... that is what
makes that worth showing.

**It is a genuine contribution.** Copies of this material are scattered across
bitsavers and the Internet Archive, but nobody has published a usable catalogue of
it -- what is on each disk, in plain language, checked against the disks
themselves. That is worth giving away, and it is the kind of thing that brings
people to the project. And falls squarely in the realm of The Research Assistant -
who is VERY good at reading!

**And it is a preservation argument.** This is orphaned, not public domain. The
safest thing we can do is ship the *tools* and let each user assemble their own
copy from an archive they obtained themselves. Yes, this is a partial cop-out --
is anyone going to notice that I redistributed this material or "made it more
available than it already is"? Probably not. Is it worth the risk when there is a
pathway that will grant the end user an "easy button" to gain maximum value for
themselves? Nope. Not even a little. I'm happy to help you fish without fishing
for you or teaching you how to fish either!

## Per-disk notes

### FOG001 - -FOG/CPM.001

> Turbo Pascal programming examples

**Note:** The first disk, and a fair sample of the whole library: Turbo Pascal source for a calendar, a loan amortiser, a Mastermind game and a cross-referencer. Nothing here is famous and nothing here is anywhere else. The lesson is that the library's value is breadth, not names.

### FOG008 - -FOG/CPM.008

> Osborne 1 Source code for BIOS and ROM You need ACT80 compiler from Sorcim to compile these files!

**Note:** **The one disk that matters to this project directly.** Osborne 1 BIOS and monitor ROM source: ROM144.ASM, OCCBIO05-95.ASM, OCCRAM15/25.ASM and RELEASE.TXT -- the letter in which Osborne permitted FOG to reprint the BIOS listing. Ten of the seventeen files are byte-identical to the copy in this repo; the rest differ only by CRLF and CP/M padding. An independent second copy of the firmware source (#203).

**Maintainer:** The firmware source, on a user-group disk, free. This is the disk that makes the O1NEXT's ROM 2.0 (#83) a reconstruction rather than a guess, and it is the reason the library belongs on the machine. Also a great way for you to experiment with adding your own features to a ROM. REMEMBER THE SIZE LIMITS THOUGH!!!!!

### FOG010 - -FOG/CPM.010

> Wordstar aids and filter programs

**Note:** WordStar aids, and a window on how much of 1985 was spent making one program behave: filters to strip WordStar's high-bit characters, to harden soft carriage returns, to convert for a CITOH Prowriter.

### FOG016 - FOG-CPM.016

> Save disk space and keep related files together with the programs on this disk. Also included in a program to extract member files from some MS-DOS.ARC or CP/M.ARK archive files.

**Note:** The library's own toolchain -- DEARC (a CP/M ARC extractor), LU/NULU and their patch files. A user group shipping the tools you need to open the user group's own disks. DEARC3.INC is one of only two members in the whole library that no available decompressor can recover.

### FOG037 - -FOG/CPM.037

> Disk Formatting & Transfer Programs & Utilities for various computers

**Note:** Disk formatting and transfer utilities for a dozen machines. With no common interchange format, 'can I read your disk' was a real question, and this disk is somebody's answer.

### FOG044 - -FOG/CPM.044

> Electric Easel drawing program and O1 arrow key utility Osborne 1 Version

**Note:** Electric Easel, a drawing program, plus **FLIPKEYS** by Curt Edwards (SoftStone Technologies, 11/11/85, "All Rights Reserved"). The Osborne 1 *has* arrow keys -- they are in the keyboard matrix -- but CP/M and WordStar disagree about what they send, and FLIPKEYS flips the layout between the two conventions. A whole copyrighted utility to settle an argument between two pieces of software about a keyboard the machine already had.

### FOG058 - -FOG/CPM.058

> Osborne Executive and CPM+ programs

**Note:** Osborne Executive and CP/M+ material. The Executive replaced the Osborne 1, and the library follows the users across.

### FOG079 - -FOG/CPM.079

> dBase II programs FOGHORN subject database index

**Note:** dBASE II programs, including a **FOGHORN subject index** -- the user group's own newsletter, indexed in its own database program.

### FOG105 - -FOG/CPM.105

> File listing programs

**Note:** The recovery case. TYPELZ13.Z80 (TYPELZ v1.3, 4 July 1986) fails to decompress under unar with a bad checksum; lbrate recovers it cleanly. It is the reason a second decompressor was needed at all.

### FOG111 - FOG-CPM.111

> This disk contains tools for Z80 and Fortran-80 programmers. Z80ASM is an assembler, ZZSOURCE is a disassembler, and CPMLIB is a collection of CP/M routines for Fortran-80.

**Note:** A programmer's toolbox: Z80ASM, ZZSOURCE (a disassembler), CPMLIB and Fortran-80 material. Assemblers for a 1976 CPU, on a 100 KB floppy.

### FOG114 - FOG-CPM.114

> Conservation of disk space is of primary interest to all users, especially when archival copies of files and programs are involved. Crunch and Archive are capable of saving as much as 70% (especially for fixed length data files). Unfortunately, the designers of these programs cannot all agree on the compression technique. The result is some incompatibility between similar but different programs or even different versions of the same program. The major difference between CRunch and ARChive is two-fold. ARChive compresses files and collects them under a single filename (usually using the extension .ARK for CP/M files and .ARC for MS-DOS files) while CRunch only compresses individual files (and replaces the middle character of the extension with a "Z"). ARChive, both because it collects files together and because there are many utilities which ease selective extraction is preferred to CRunch. The problem is that we cannot yet easily (and quickly) create ARChive files on CP/M systems -- it must be done on an MS-DOS computer. PLEASE do not use CRunch on files which you will submit to the FOG disk library or the FOG publications. Only upload CRunched files to a remote system if you have gotten approval from the SysOp -- many do not allow CRunched files on their systems.

**Note:** 'Conservation of disk space is of primary interest to all users.' The whole era in one line: every design decision on every disk here was made inside a **182 KB** budget -- the capacity of a double-density Osborne disk, and double what a single-density machine gave you a year earlier.

**Maintainer:** The sentence that explains the whole library. I want a newcomer to read it and realise that '182 KB' was not a limitation people complained about -- it was the design brief. AND it was double the size it was only a short time ago (if you had the double density upgrade!)

**Note:** IMP244, a communications program by Irv Hoff, with overlays. The overlay system exists because you could not fit support for every modem on one disk.

### FOG158 - --FOGCPM.158

> Commodore C128 programs

**Note:** **Commodore C-128 programs**, on a disk in the *First Osborne* Group library. By the late 1980s FOG had outlived the machine it was named for and become a general CP/M users' group -- and the library's composition over time shows exactly that.

**Maintainer:** C-128 programs in the *Osborne* library. This is the fact I would put in front of a newcomer first: the group outlived the machine. It makes the whole thing a human story rather than a parts catalogue. And underscores how brutally under appreciated the C128 is. I love my C64 Ultimate, but a C128 Ultimate would be EPIC.

**Note:** JRT Pascal 4.0, disk 2 of 4. A complete commercial Pascal compiler across four disks, preserved after the vendor was gone.

**Note:** DazzleStar, moved from FOG-CPM.017 because it had grown too large to share a disk -- the size constraint shaping the library's own filing.

> John Wyatt's Future Systems Double Sided Double Density disk drive upgrade for the Osborne Executive. Do not attempt this upgrade until you have read ALL of the instructions or you will cause yourself great grief.

**Note:** **John Wyatt's Future Systems double-sided double-density upgrade for the Osborne Executive** -- installation notes for a hardware modification, preserved in the software library. Relevant to #95.

**Maintainer:** A hardware mod's install notes, in the software library. When we ship the interposer, this is the company we are in.

### FOG192 - FOG-CPM.192

> Disk utilities not really intended for the novice user. Be sure to read the documentation carefully and completely as well as making a COMPLETE and CURRENT backup of your system before using these utilities, especially FIRE12 (which does not appear to work on anything other than a Morrow MD 11).

**Note:** 'Disk utilities not really intended for the novice user. Be sure to read the documentation carefully and completely.' The library assumes competence and says so, which is itself a period attitude.

> A collection of aids for the Kaypro user, aimed primarily at Perfect Software products (especially Perfect Writer).

**Note:** Aids for the **Kaypro** user, aimed at Perfect Writer. The Kaypro was the Osborne 1's closest competitor; a FOG member wrote for both, and the library kept both.

### FOG204 - FOG-CPM.204

> A collection of Digital Research application notes with fixes for some of the utility programs plus some utilities for the "heavy modem" user. Also on this disk is a list of terminal control codes.

**Note:** Digital Research application notes and fixes -- primary-source material from the company that wrote CP/M, redistributed by a user group.

### FOG205 - FOG-CPM.205

> Disk 1 of 4. ZCPR 3. The ZCPR on these disks is the last public domain version. For future enhancements, you must buy the commercial version (NZ-COM for CP/M 2.2 systems or Z3PLUS for CPM+ systems). **Note:** ZCPR 3, disk 1 of 4. ZCPR is the enhanced CP/M command processor that kept CP/M alive long after Digital Research stopped caring. Four disks of it, including the note that this is the last public-domain version and later ones must be bought: a community's OS politics, on floppies.

### FOG213 - FOG-CPM.213

> Disk 1 of 2: Family Ties. For those who may one day move to MS/PC-DOS, all data entered with this version can be used by the MS/PC-DOS version. It is not likely that there will be further upgrades to this program. Be sure to check the placement of the files which depends on the capacity of your disks: the appendix has complete information.

**Note:** Family Ties: a database that can hand its data to MS/PC-DOS. The library documenting its own platform's decline, in a file-format decision.
